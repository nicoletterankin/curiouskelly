### CASE STUDY 1: Emma's New Hand (e-NABLE Prosthetic)

**Real Story:** e-NABLE Community
**Theme:** Open-source, accessibility, democratization, medical innovation
**Emotional Arc:** Problem → Community solution → Empowerment

---

#### Full Story (Adaptable by Age)

**The Problem:**
Emma is a 7-year-old girl who was born without fingers on her left hand. Traditional prosthetic hands cost $10,000-$50,000, and kids outgrow them quickly. Emma's family couldn't afford one, and even if they could, she'd need a new one every year or two as she grows.

**The Solution:**
A global network of volunteers called e-NABLE uses 3D printers to make prosthetic hands for kids like Emma. Volunteers can download free designs from the internet, print the parts on a 3D printer, and assemble them. The materials cost less than $50.

**How It Started:**
In 2011, a man named Ivan Owen made a metal hand for a costume and posted a video on YouTube. A carpenter from South Africa named Richard Van As, who had lost his fingers in an accident, saw the video and reached out. Together, they figured out how to use 3D printing to make prosthetic hands. They shared their designs for free so anyone in the world could make them.

**The Impact:**
- Over 200 e-NABLE chapters in 100+ countries
- Thousands of people have received hands
- Cost: Under $50 vs. $10,000+
- Kids can customize colors and designs (superhero themes, favorite colors, etc.)
- When they outgrow it, they can print a bigger one
- Designs keep improving as the community shares ideas

**Emma's Experience:**
Emma got to pick her favorite colors—purple and silver. A local makerspace printed the parts, and volunteers assembled it with her family. Now she can ride her bike better, hold onto monkey bars, and carry her backpack. Best of all, when she outgrows it in a year, they'll just print a bigger one.

---

#### Age-Specific Adaptations:

**Ages 2-5:**
"Emma is a kid who needed a special hand. Nice people used a magic building machine to make her one! They put in purple and silver, her favorite colors. Now Emma can hold things and play. And when she grows bigger, they'll make her a bigger hand. Isn't that cool?"

**Ages 6-12:**
Focus on Emma's story, the problem-solving, and the community. "Imagine if you or your friend needed something important but it cost too much money. That's what happened with Emma and prosthetic hands. But then people around the world worked together..." Emphasize the sharing and collaboration.

**Ages 13-17:**
Full story with emphasis on the injustice of $50,000 prosthetics and how open-source design challenges corporate medical device pricing. Discuss how IP and patents affect healthcare access. Empower them: "You could be part of e-NABLE too."

**Ages 18+:**
Full context including economics of medical devices, patent system, FDA regulation vs. open innovation, global health equity, and systems change. Connect to broader right-to-healthcare debates.

### CASE STUDY 2: The Printed Neighborhood (ICON Homes)

**Real Story:** Wolf Ranch & Community First! Village, Texas
**Theme:** Affordable housing, homelessness, sustainability, scalability
**Emotional Arc:** Crisis → Innovation → Community transformation

---

#### Full Story (Adaptable by Age)

**The Problem:**
Housing is expensive and millions of people don't have homes. Traditional construction is slow, wastes materials, and costs a lot. In Austin, Texas, many people experiencing homelessness needed safe, affordable housing.

**The Solution:**
A company called ICON created a giant 3D printer—bigger than a house—that can print the walls of a home in about 8 days. They use a special concrete-like material that comes out of a nozzle and builds the walls layer by layer, just like a regular 3D printer but HUGE.

**The Details:**
- Build time: 8 days
- Strength: 350% stronger than traditional houses
- Energy efficiency: 45-60% more efficient (lower heating/cooling bills)
- Cost: Fraction of traditional construction
- Waste: Much less than traditional building (only uses material needed)

**Community First! Village Project:**
ICON is building 100 homes for people who've been experiencing chronic homelessness. The homes range from 380 to 1,040 square feet. They're safe, strong, energy-efficient, and affordable. The project is creating a community where people can rebuild their lives.

**Wolf Ranch Neighborhood:**
ICON and Lennar built a whole 100-home neighborhood using 3D printing. Real families live there now. It proves that 3D printing isn't just for prototypes—it can build at scale.

**The Technology Evolution (2024):**
ICON announced the Phoenix printer that can print multi-story buildings and reduced their costs by 50%. They're now taking orders starting at $25 per square foot for walls, or $80 per square foot including foundation and roof. Compare to traditional construction: $150-$300 per square foot.

**Environmental Benefit:**
They developed CarbonX, a low-carbon building material. Research from MIT showed that 3D-printed homes have lower environmental impact than stick-framed construction—both in materials used and energy to run the home.

---

#### Age-Specific Adaptations:

**Ages 2-5:**
"Some people don't have homes. That's sad. But there's a GIANT robot printer—as big as a house!—that can build houses by squeezing out special mud and stacking it up layer by layer. It builds a house in just 8 days! And the houses are strong and cozy. Now lots of people have homes because of the building robot."

**Ages 6-12:**
Focus on the scale and wow-factor. "Imagine a 3D printer so big it could print your whole house! That's what ICON made. It works the same way as a regular 3D printer, but instead of plastic, it uses special concrete. They're building whole neighborhoods and homes for people who need them. The houses are stronger, use less energy, and are better for the Earth."

**Ages 13-17:**
Full story with emphasis on the housing crisis and how technology can address social problems. Discuss affordability, homelessness, sustainability. Ask critical questions: "Is this scalable globally? What about places without electricity? Could this technology be controlled by a few companies, or should it be open-source like e-NABLE? What would it take to print affordable homes in every community?"

**Ages 18+:**
Complete economic analysis. Compare costs, discuss scalability, explore policy implications (zoning, building codes, unions, job displacement vs. creation). Environmental lifecycle analysis. Financing and ownership models. International applications for disaster relief and development. Business model questions: Should this be a for-profit company or a public utility?

### CASE STUDY 3: The Broken Espresso Machine (Right to Repair)

**Real Story:** Groupe SEB / Philips Fixables
**Theme:** Right to repair, planned obsolescence, consumer empowerment, waste reduction
**Emotional Arc:** Frustration → Discovery → Empowerment

---

#### Full Story (Adaptable by Age)

**The Problem:**
Maria's espresso machine broke. A tiny plastic drip collector cracked. The machine still worked perfectly otherwise—it was just this one little part. She called the company: "Can I buy a replacement part?" They said: "Sorry, we don't make that part anymore. You'll have to buy a whole new machine." A new machine costs $200. The broken part probably costs 50 cents to make.

This happens all the time with electronics, appliances, and gadgets. Companies stop making parts, or make it impossible to get them, so you have to throw away the whole thing and buy new. It's called "planned obsolescence."

**The Old Way:**
- Throw away the whole machine
- Buy a new one for $200
- The old one goes to a landfill
- Company makes more money
- You lose money and the planet gets more waste

**The New Way (3D Printing + Right to Repair):**
**The Solution:**

Groupe SEB (the company that made Maria's machine) worked with Ricoh 3D to create a 3D-printable version of the discontinued part. Now anyone can:
1. Download the design file for free
2. 3D print the part (costs maybe $1-2 in materials)
3. Install it themselves
4. Keep using their machine for years

Groupe SEB now offers 3D-printed spare parts for several products as part of a 10-year repair guarantee.

**Other Companies Joining In:**
- **Philips** launched "Philips Fixables"—3D-printable replacement parts for their products
- **Framework** Computer shares 3D-printable models for laptop parts
- **Raspberry Pi** and **Adafruit** provide printable components

**The Legal Push:**
**European Union (2021):** Companies must make spare parts available for 10 years and can't block repairs with 3D-printed or second-hand parts.

**United States:** Several states passed Right to Repair laws requiring companies to provide access to parts, tools, and manuals.

**Why This Matters:**
- Reduces electronic waste (WEEE)
- Saves consumers money
- Extends product lifespan
- Challenges corporate control
- Empowers people to fix their own stuff
- Better for the environment

**Maria's Outcome:**
Maria found the 3D printable file, went to her local library's makerspace, printed the part for $2, and fixed her espresso machine. It took 30 minutes. Her machine works perfectly and she saved $200. Plus she learned how to 3D print and now she's thinking about what else she could make and fix.

---

#### Age-Specific Adaptations:

**Ages 2-5:**
"Sometimes toys or things at home break. Just a tiny piece breaks, but the rest is fine. Before, you had to throw the whole thing away. But now, grown-ups can use a 3D printer to make the broken piece! Then the toy works again. That's better than throwing things away, right?"

**Ages 6-12:**
Tell simplified Maria story. "Have you ever had a toy or something break and your family couldn't fix it? That's frustrating! But now, if something breaks, we can use a 3D printer to make the part that broke. Companies are starting to share the designs so people can fix their own stuff. That means less trash and we get to keep things we love instead of throwing them away."

**Ages 13-17:**
Full Maria story with emphasis on planned obsolescence and corporate practices. "Companies sometimes make it hard to repair things on purpose so you have to buy new products. But now laws are changing and 3D printing makes it possible to create replacement parts ourselves. This is called the Right to Repair movement. What do you think—should companies be required to share designs for replacement parts?"

**Ages 18+:**
Complete context: economics of planned obsolescence, IP/patent issues, environmental impact of e-waste, Right to Repair legislation in EU and US states, corporate resistance, consumer advocacy, and the role of 3D printing in enabling a circular economy. Discuss business model implications: should companies profit from repairs or from making long-lasting products?

## Storytelling Guidelines

### For All Ages:

**Start with the Human:**
Begin with a person and their problem, not the technology. We care about Emma needing a hand, not about FDM printing parameters.

**Show the Tension:**
Present the problem (cost, access, waste, broken things) before the solution. Create emotional investment.

**Make It Real:**
Use specific details: Emma picked purple and silver. Maria's espresso machine cost $200 to replace. The house printer is taller than a giraffe.

**Connect to Learner's Life:**
- Ages 2-5: Toys, colors, helping friends
- Ages 6-12: Broken things, school projects, pets, sports equipment
- Ages 13-17: Money, fairness, climate, future
- Ages 18+: Bills, work, community, systems

**Show Agency:**
End with empowerment. Even if learner doesn't have a printer, they can: visit makerspaces, design on Tinkercad, join e-NABLE, support Right to Repair, share open-source designs, teach others.

---

### Age-Specific Storytelling:

**Ages 2-5:**
- Short (2-3 minutes)
- Sensory language (colors, textures, sounds)
- Clear good (helping, sharing) and problems (broken, sad)
- Simple cause and effect
- Celebrate wonder

**Ages 6-12:**
- Story arc (problem → attempts → solution → impact)
- Concrete details they can visualize
- Relatable problems (broken toys, needing help)
- Encourage questions and imagination
- "What would YOU design?"

**Ages 13-17:**
- Real stakes (money, justice, climate)
- Multiple perspectives (not just "3D printing good")
- Critical questions (who benefits? what are the tradeoffs?)
- Empowerment (you can participate in this)
- Connect to their values

**Ages 18+:**
- Full context and nuance
- Systems thinking (economics, policy, culture)
- Practical application (what would this mean for your work/community?)
- Honest about challenges and limitations
- Strategic questions (how do we actually achieve democratization?)

## Emotional Arcs by Case Study

### Emma's Hand (e-NABLE)
1. **Problem:** Exclusion, expensive medical devices, inaccessible healthcare
2. **Discovery:** One person's costume project + one person's need = collaboration
3. **Solution:** Open-source community, 3D printing makes it affordable
4. **Impact:** Thousands of hands, 100+ countries, kids can customize and regrow
5. **Emotion:** Hope, empowerment, community care

**Key Lesson:** When we share knowledge freely, we can solve problems that seem impossible.

---

### Printed Neighborhood (ICON)
1. **Problem:** Housing crisis, homelessness, expensive slow construction, waste
2. **Innovation:** Giant robot printer, new materials, radical efficiency
3. **Proof:** Real homes, real people, whole neighborhoods
4. **Scale:** 100 homes, multi-story capability, $25-80/sq ft
5. **Emotion:** Wonder, possibility, "we can actually solve this"

**Key Lesson:** Technology at scale can address major societal challenges if we choose to deploy it for social good.

---

### Broken Espresso Machine (Right to Repair)
1. **Problem:** Frustration, waste, planned obsolescence, powerlessness
2. **Injustice:** Companies profit from breaking/blocking repairs
3. **Fightback:** Right to Repair movement, legislation, corporate policy shifts
4. **Solution:** 3D printing enables part replacement, extends product life
5. **Emotion:** Frustration → empowerment → "I can fix my own stuff"

**Key Lesson:** We don't have to accept a disposable culture. We can fight for the right to repair and use tools like 3D printing to maintain our agency.

## Usage Notes for Lesson DNA Development

- **Mix examples within age groups:** Don't use just one story. Variety maintains interest.
- **Adapt complexity, not core message:** The message (open-source, community, empowerment) stays constant; the framing changes.
- **Use primary cases as anchors:** Always include at least one of the three core cases.
- **Connect to learner experience:** Find the hook that relates to their lives.
- **End with empowerment:** Always give learners a way to engage, even without equipment.
